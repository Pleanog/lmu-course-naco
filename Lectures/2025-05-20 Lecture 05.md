
## Quantum Computing

Quantum computing represents a radical shift in how we process information. Rather than relying on classical binary states (0 or 1), quantum computers utilize principles from quantum mechanics — most notably **superposition** and **entanglement** — to encode and manipulate data in fundamentally different ways.

---

## Classical vs. Quantum States

In classical physics, a particle may have a **spin** in one of two directions: for example, **left** or **right**. At any given time, the particle _has_ a definite spin, even if we haven't yet measured it. Once we measure the spin, we simply reveal a property that was already there.

Superposition describes a quantum system that exists in **a combination of all possible states** simultaneously — until it is measured.

> 🔍 **Quantum Superposition:**  
> In quantum mechanics, superposition refers to a system existing in **multiple states at once**. When a measurement is made, the system "collapses" into one of the possible states.
### Superposition vs. Classical Uncertainty

Unlike classical uncertainty (where we don’t know which state a system is in), a quantum system in superposition is **genuinely in all the states at once** — mathematically modeled as a weighted sum (linear combination) of those states.

> [!TLDR] Superposition:  
> It’s not just a lack of knowledge about the system — it’s a real, physical state where **both possibilities exist until observed**.

---

## Measurement in Quantum Systems

Measurement in quantum systems **doesn’t just observe the state** — it **changes it**. Upon measurement, the quantum system collapses into a specific state, and this collapse is irreversible.

![[Pasted image 20250714212148.png]]

> [!CITE] Quantum Measurement Principle  
> "Once a quantum state is measured, its superposition collapses into a single outcome — and this choice defines the state from then on."

### Example: Measuring Spin

Mathematically, a quantum particle is in both states (left / right spin) simultaneously. But once we measure it, we get **only one result** — either left **or** right. The act of measurement **forces** the particle into that state.


![[Pasted image 20250520132508.png]]


> [!question] _What happens to a quantum system when it is measured?_  
> The system's superposition collapses to one of the possible basis states. From that point on, further measurements return the same result.

---

## Mathematical Representation of Quantum States

Quantum states are typically represented using **Dirac notation**, also called **bra-ket notation**. This is notational shorthand that helps describe quantum vectors and operations between them.

> 🔍 **Dirac Notation:**
> - `|x⟩` is called a **ket**, representing a column vector (state).
> - `⟨x|` is a **bra**, representing a row vector (dual state).
> - Together, `⟨x|y⟩` represents an **inner product**.

Example:

> q = ∑ cₓ · |x⟩ for x ∈ X

This is a superposition of basis states `|x⟩`, each weighted by a complex coefficient `cₓ`.

> [!TIP] Kets and Meaning  
> The ket `|x⟩` carries no inherent semantics; it's simply a notational tool that emphasizes we're working in a **Hilbert space**, the abstract space used in quantum mechanics.

---

## Superposition vs. Parallelism: Important Distinction

Superposition is often confused with **parallel computation**, but the analogy is misleading.

> [!TIP] Superposition is NOT Parallel Computing  
> In classical parallel computing, multiple computations happen independently at the same time. In contrast, quantum superposition is a **linear combination of states**, not a set of parallel processes. Measurement yields only **one result**, not all.

> [!question] _Is quantum superposition equivalent to running all computations in parallel?_  
> No. Superposition allows many possibilities to **exist simultaneously**, but once measured, only **one outcome** is observed. There's no simultaneous output of all results.

---

## Entanglement

> 🔍 **Quantum Entanglement:**  
> When two particles are entangled, the state of one **immediately affects** the state of the other — even if separated by large distances. This nonlocal behavior is essential in quantum algorithms and quantum cryptography.

> [!TIP] Entanglement Analogy  
> Imagine you and a friend each have a pair of gloves — one left, one right — but don’t know which. If you open yours and see it's a right glove, you instantly know your friend has the left one, even if they’re across the world.

> [!example] Klausurrelevant: Explain how entanglement differs from classical correlation.

> [!TIP] Quantum vs. Classical Interpretation of State  
> In classical systems, unknown = uncertain. In quantum systems, unknown = **genuinely undefined** until measurement.

> [!question] _Why does measurement destroy superposition?_  
> The act of measuring "selects" one basis state from the superposition. According to the Copenhagen interpretation, this interaction with the environment "collapses" the quantum wavefunction.
